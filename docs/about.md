# What is this website?

This is a small collection of plain-language guides to everyday cybersecurity: password managers, two-factor authentication, passkeys, software updates, scams, and spam text messages. It is published by Admin2x, LLC, and it is free to read and share.

## Who we are

Admin2x is a security consultancy that tests artificial intelligence systems for the companies that build them: applications built on large language models, AI agents, and the tools and integrations they connect to. That is the whole business.

Admin2x does not provide personal tech support, IT services, or incident response for individuals or households, and nothing on this site is an offer of those services. If you are here about AI security work, [admin2x.com](https://admin2x.com/){target=_blank} is the place to start.

## So why does an AI security firm publish guides about password managers?

Because anyone who works in security ends up being asked these questions, by all kinds of people, in all kinds of settings, and the honest answers turn out to be the same handful of habits nearly every time. Those answers deserve to be written down carefully, once, with links to the official instructions, rather than explained in a hurry over the phone, a little differently each time.

There is also a professional reason. Much of the damage seen in security work, at large companies and at home alike, still starts with something basic: a reused password, a skipped update, a convincing phone call. The habits in these guides are the same ones that matter in our day-to-day work, just written for a kitchen table instead of a conference room.

## How the guides are written

The guides are written to be as vendor-neutral as practical. Where one names a specific product, it is because that product is a reasonable default for most people, not because anyone paid for the mention; there are no ads, no affiliate links, and nothing for sale here. Every set of steps links to the official instructions from the company or government agency involved, so you can check our advice against the source. Each guide notes when it was last reviewed, because phone and computer menus change often.

The guides focus on Mac, iPhone, and iPad, with Android covered where it matters. They are written for people in the United States, so phone carriers, reporting agencies, and laws mentioned are American ones.

## What this site is not

There is no help desk, contact form, or support channel behind this website, and we are not able to take requests for individual help through it. If you need help right now, these are the right places to go:

For a problem with an Apple device, contact Apple Support yourself, through the Apple Support app or Apple's website, never through a phone number shown in a pop-up or given to you by a caller. For a problem with a bank account, card, or payment app, call the number on the back of your card or use the institution's own app. To report a scam, use the Federal Trade Commission's [ReportFraud.ftc.gov](https://reportfraud.ftc.gov/){target=_blank}. If your identity has been stolen, [IdentityTheft.gov](https://www.identitytheft.gov/){target=_blank} builds a step-by-step recovery plan. And to talk through a scam with a person, the AARP Fraud Watch Network runs a free helpline at 877-908-3360 that anyone can call; you do not need to be an AARP member.

If something is happening right now, start with [Recognize and stop phishing and scams](articles/phishing-and-scams.md), which begins with what to do in the moment.
