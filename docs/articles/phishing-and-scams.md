# Recognize and stop phishing and scams

!!! danger "Is this happening to you right now?"
    **Stop.** Hang up the phone, close the browser tab, or put the message down. A real bank, company, or government agency will still be there in ten minutes; a scammer depends on you not stopping. Do not pay anything, do not read any code to anyone, do not install anything, and do not let anyone control your computer. Then call your bank using the number printed on the back of your card, not any number you were given. If you have already paid, clicked, or given out information, go straight to [If you already clicked, paid, or gave information](#if-you-already-clicked-paid-or-gave-information).

!!! info "Last reviewed October 29, 2026"
    Scam scripts change constantly, but the patterns in this guide have stayed the same for years. Figures are from the FBI's 2025 Internet Crime Report.

## The short version

Almost every scam starts the same way: someone contacts you. A call, a text, an email, a pop-up on your screen, a message from a stranger. They claim to be your bank, Apple, Amazon, the government, a delivery company, or a family member, and they say something is wrong and you need to act quickly.

The single habit that defeats nearly all of them is this: **never act on contact you did not start.** If a call, text, email, or pop-up says there is a problem, do not respond to it, do not click it, and do not call the number it gives you. Instead, end the contact and reach the company or person yourself, using a phone number, website, or app you already know is real: the number on the back of your card, the app already on your phone, the website you type in yourself, the contact already saved in your phone. If there really is a problem, they will know about it when you call. If there is not, you have just avoided a scam.

There are also four requests that are always a scam, no matter who seems to be asking or how convincing they are:

1. Paying with **gift cards, cryptocurrency, a cryptocurrency ATM, a wire transfer, or cash** to fix a problem, avoid a penalty, or protect your money.
2. **Reading back a verification code** that was just sent to your phone or email.
3. **Moving your money to a "safe" account** to protect it, or withdrawing cash for someone to pick up.
4. **Installing an app or letting someone control your computer** to fix a problem you did not know you had.

If you remember nothing else from this page, remember the habit and those four.

## Why this matters more than anything else on this site

The other guides on this site protect you from attacks against your accounts and devices. Scams go around all of that by targeting you directly, and they work: the FBI's [2025 Internet Crime Report](https://www.ic3.gov/AnnualReport/Reports/2025_IC3Report.pdf){target=_blank} counted nearly $21 billion in reported losses in a single year, and people aged 60 and over reported losing about $7.7 billion, more than any other age group. Tech support scams alone accounted for more than $2 billion.

The people running these scams are not lone amateurs. Many work in organized operations with scripts, shifts, and managers. They can make their phone number appear on your caller ID as your bank's real number or Apple's real number, a trick called spoofing that Apple specifically [warns about](https://support.apple.com/en-us/102568){target=_blank}. They often know your name, address, and which bank you use, from data breaches. And the old advice to look for bad spelling and grammar no longer works; the U.S. Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency [notes](https://www.cisa.gov/secure-our-world/recognize-and-report-phishing){target=_blank} that artificial intelligence now lets scammers write polished, convincing messages.

None of that means you are helpless. It means you should not try to judge whether a particular call or message is real by how real it looks or sounds. Judge it by whether you started the contact. That test cannot be faked.

## The scams you are most likely to see

### The fake bank or fraud-department call

This is one of the most common serious scams right now. Your phone rings, and the caller ID shows your bank's name, or Apple, or Amazon. A calm, professional voice says they have detected suspicious activity on your account, perhaps a large purchase you did not make, and they want to help you stop it.

From there, the call goes one of two ways. In the first version, they say they need to "verify your identity" and ask you to read them a code that was just texted to you. That code was sent because they are, at that moment, logging in to your account as you; reading it to them hands them the account. The [two-factor guide](two-factor-authentication.md#what-two-factor-authentication-does-not-protect-against) explains this in more detail. In the second version, they say your account has been compromised and you need to move your money to a new, secure account to protect it, or withdraw cash, or buy gold, while staying on the phone with them. They may tell you not to tell the bank teller why, because the teller might be "part of the fraud." The Federal Trade Commission [describes this exact script](https://consumer.ftc.gov/consumer-alerts/2024/01/did-someone-tell-you-move-or-transfer-your-money-it-could-be-scam){target=_blank}; anyone who tells you to lie to your bank about why you are moving money is a scammer.

**What to do:** hang up. It does not matter how real it sounds or what the caller ID says. Then call the number on the back of your bank card, or open your bank's app yourself, and ask whether there is a problem. Your bank will never ask you for a verification code, never ask for your password or PIN, and never tell you to move your money to protect it.

### The tech support pop-up or call

You are browsing the web and a page suddenly fills your screen, maybe with a loud alarm or a voice, saying your computer is infected, your files are at risk, or your Apple or Microsoft account is locked. It shows a phone number to call immediately. Or someone calls you, claiming to be from Apple or Microsoft, saying they have detected a problem with your computer.

Every part of this is fake. Apple and Microsoft do not put phone numbers in warning pop-ups and do not call you about problems with your computer. If you call the number, you reach a scammer who will ask you to install a remote-control app so they can "fix" the problem, then show you fake evidence of infection, and then ask for payment, often by gift card or cryptocurrency, or ask you to log in to your bank so they can "process a refund." The FTC's guide [How To Spot, Avoid, and Report Tech Support Scams](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/how-spot-avoid-and-report-tech-support-scams){target=_blank} walks through the whole script.

**What to do:** do not call the number. Close the browser tab or quit the browser entirely (on a Mac, press **Command-Q** while the browser is in front; if it will not quit, choose **Force Quit** from the Apple menu). The scary page cannot do anything on its own; it only works if you call. If you are worried something is actually wrong with your computer, open a ticket. A related trick is a pop-up telling you your browser or Mac "needs an update"; [the updates guide](software-updates.md#what-not-to-bother-with-and-what-to-avoid) explains why that is always fake.

### The family emergency call

A call comes from a grandchild, child, or other relative, sometimes crying, sometimes speaking fast. They have been in a car accident, or arrested, or are stranded somewhere, and they need money right now. Often a second person comes on the line claiming to be a lawyer or police officer. They ask you not to tell anyone else in the family. They want payment by wire, gift card, or cash that someone will pick up from your door.

The voice may sound exactly like your relative. Scammers can now clone a person's voice from short audio clips, such as videos posted on social media, and the FTC [has warned](https://consumer.ftc.gov/scams/family-emergency-scams){target=_blank} that they are using it to make these calls more convincing.

**What to do:** hang up and call your relative back on the number you already have for them, or call another family member who would know. Then set up a defense before you ever need it. The FBI [recommends](https://www.ic3.gov/psa/2024/PSA241203){target=_blank} creating a secret word or phrase with your family that a real family member in trouble would know and a scammer would not. Pick one this week, something that is not on social media, and make sure the older and younger members of your family both know it. If a caller cannot give it, hang up.

### Phishing texts and emails

These are the messages about a package that could not be delivered, an unpaid highway toll, a locked account, a suspicious login, a refund you are owed, or an invoice for something you did not buy. They all contain a link or a phone number, and they all want you to act now. The link leads to a fake website that looks exactly like the real one and collects whatever you type into it: your password, your card number, your Social Security number.

The FTC's guide [How To Recognize and Avoid Phishing Scams](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/how-recognize-and-avoid-phishing-scams){target=_blank} lists the common versions, and Apple's guide [Recognize and avoid social engineering schemes](https://support.apple.com/en-us/102568){target=_blank} shows what fake Apple messages look like.

**What to do:** do not tap the link and do not call the number in the message. If you think the message might be real, open the company's app or type its website address yourself and check your account there. Then delete the message, and report it if your phone or email offers a "report junk" or "report phishing" option. Our guide on [stopping spam text messages](stop-spam-texts.md) covers how to filter most of these out before you see them. And if you are using passkeys, as described in [the passkeys guide](passkeys.md), a fake website cannot steal your login at all, because your passkey will not work on a site with the wrong address.

### Investment, romance, and "wrong number" scams

These start slowly and are designed to feel like a real relationship. A text arrives that seems to be a wrong number ("Hi, is this Jenny? Are we still on for dinner?"), and when you politely reply, the sender apologizes, is friendly, and keeps chatting. Or someone you met on social media or a dating app becomes a close online friend or partner over weeks. Eventually, they mention how well they have done investing in cryptocurrency, and offer to show you how, on a website or app they recommend. The site shows your investment growing. When you try to withdraw it, there are fees, then taxes, then more fees, and the money is gone.

These are the most expensive scams of all: the FBI counted more than $8 billion in investment-fraud losses in 2025, much of it through cryptocurrency.

**What to do:** treat this as a firm rule. Anyone you have met online or by text, and have not met in person, who brings up investing or cryptocurrency is a scammer. There are no exceptions. Stop responding.

### Anyone who asks for gift cards

This deserves its own heading because it appears in almost every other scam. The FTC's rule is short and complete: [gift cards are only for gifts](https://consumer.ftc.gov/consumer-alerts/2024/02/check-out-line-buying-gift-cards-read-avoid-scam){target=_blank}. No real business, government agency, utility, court, or tech support service will ever ask you to pay with a gift card, and anyone who asks you to read them the numbers off the back of one is stealing from you.

## Things real organizations never do

Here is a short list worth keeping in your head. Apple publishes most of it in its [own guidance](https://support.apple.com/en-us/102568){target=_blank}, and it applies equally to your bank, Amazon, Google, Microsoft, and the government.

A real organization will never ask you to read back a verification code, never ask for your password or device passcode, never ask you to turn off two-factor authentication or any other security feature, never ask you to pay with gift cards or cryptocurrency, never tell you to move money to protect it, never ask you to keep the call secret from family or bank staff, and never pressure you to act before you can hang up and call them back. If someone contacting you does any of these things, you have your answer, whatever the caller ID says.

## If you already clicked, paid, or gave information

First: this happens to careful, intelligent people every day, and the scams are designed by professionals specifically to work on them. Acting quickly matters far more than anything else, so do not let embarrassment slow you down. The steps below follow the FTC's guide [What To Do if You Were Scammed](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/what-do-if-you-were-scammed){target=_blank}. Do them in this order, because the first one is time-sensitive.

### Step 1: If money left your account, call now

Call the institution that moved the money, using the number on the back of your card or in its official app, and tell them you were scammed and want the payment stopped or reversed. Minutes and hours matter here.

If you paid with a **credit or debit card**, call the card issuer and dispute the charge. If you sent a **bank transfer or Zelle** payment, call your bank. If you used **Venmo, Cash App, or PayPal**, report it in the app and ask for a reversal. If you paid with **gift cards**, call the company that issued the card, using the number on the card, tell them it was used in a scam, and keep the card and the receipt. If you sent a **wire transfer** through Western Union or MoneyGram, call them and report it as fraud. If you sent **cryptocurrency**, contact the exchange or the crypto ATM operator; recovery is unlikely, but report it anyway. If you mailed **cash**, call the U.S. Postal Inspection Service at 1-877-876-2455, which can sometimes intercept a package that has not yet been delivered.

### Step 2: If you gave someone control of your computer

Disconnect the computer from the internet right away: turn off Wi-Fi, and unplug the network cable if there is one. Do not use that computer to log in to your bank, email, or anything else until it has been checked. Then, from a different device you trust, such as your phone, change the passwords for your email and bank accounts and confirm two-factor authentication is on, as described in [the two-factor guide](two-factor-authentication.md). Then open a ticket with the subject line URGENT, as described in [How to get help](../how-to-get-help.md).

### Step 3: If you gave out a password or verification code

Change that password immediately, from a device you trust. If you used the same password anywhere else, change it there too, which is the reason [the password-reuse guide](password-reuse.md) exists. Check that two-factor authentication is still turned on and that the phone number and email address on the account are still yours; a common move after a takeover is to change them so you cannot get back in. If you cannot log in at all, use the company's account recovery process right away.

### Step 4: If you gave out your Social Security number or other identity information

Go to [IdentityTheft.gov](https://www.identitytheft.gov/){target=_blank}, the FTC's identity-theft recovery site, which builds a step-by-step recovery plan for your specific situation. It will walk you through placing a fraud alert or freezing your credit with the three credit bureaus, which prevents anyone from opening new accounts in your name.

### Step 5: Report it

Report the scam to the FTC at [ReportFraud.ftc.gov](https://reportfraud.ftc.gov/){target=_blank} and to the FBI at [ic3.gov](https://www.ic3.gov/){target=_blank}. Reports rarely get money back on their own, but they are how these operations get investigated and shut down, and your bank or card company may ask for a report number.

### Step 6: Watch out for the second scam

After people lose money, they are often contacted by someone offering to recover it, for a fee: a "recovery service," a lawyer, a government agency, or a "crypto tracing" firm. These are scammers too, often the same ones, working from a list of people who have already paid once. No legitimate agency charges you to recover stolen money. Ignore anyone who contacts you offering this.

If you are 50 or older, or helping someone who is, the AARP Fraud Watch Network runs a free helpline at 877-908-3360.

## Set up your family before it happens

Most of the defenses on this page work best if they are agreed in advance, not in the middle of a frightening call. Take ten minutes with the people closest to you, especially older parents and grandparents, and agree on three things: a family code word that a real relative in trouble would know; that anyone in the family who gets an urgent call about another family member will hang up and call that person directly before doing anything; and that nobody in the family will ever pay anyone with gift cards, cryptocurrency, or cash pickups, for any reason. Then put this page's address somewhere they will find it.

## The rest of this site makes scams less damaging

A scam that gets your password does far less damage if that password is not used anywhere else, which is what [the password manager guide](password-manager.md) sets up. A scam that gets your password still fails if two-factor authentication is on, as [the two-factor guide](two-factor-authentication.md) describes, and a fake login page fails entirely against [a passkey](passkeys.md). A malicious link is far less likely to work on a Mac and browser that are [up to date](software-updates.md). None of those replace the habit at the top of this page, but together they mean that a single mistake, which happens to everyone eventually, is an inconvenience rather than a disaster.
